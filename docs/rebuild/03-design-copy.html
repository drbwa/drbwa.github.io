§ 1

Direction

The brief is "modern but simplistic", which is also the brief most reliably ruined by enthusiasm. So the direction here is deliberately narrow: a quiet reading surface with one accent colour and no boxes.

Almost everything on the site is text, and text is what the design is for. There is a single column, a hairline rule system instead of cards, one accent used only for links and the current-page marker, and a serif face doing the reading work because a researcher's site should look like something written rather than something shipped. The restraint is the identity — anything added on top makes it look more like every other generated portfolio, not less.

The one signature detail is a monospace metadata line: the role under your name, the date and reading time under a post title, the technology list under a CV entry. It is the only place the mono face appears outside code, and it is what makes the pages feel like a lab notebook rather than a brochure.

Do
  • One column, ~68 characters wide, generous line height.
  • Hairline rules to separate; whitespace to group.
  • Monospace for dates, tech lists, labels, code — nowhere else.
  • Let figures and tables break out wider than the text column.
  • Underline links. Always. Colour alone is not enough.
  • Both themes designed, plus the un-stamped follow-the-OS state.
Do not
  • Cards, panels, tiles, or any grid of boxes.
  • Gradients, shadows, glows, blurs, radii above 2px.
  • A hero section, a photograph of a person, an avatar.
  • Icon sets or emoji as section markers.
  • Skill bars, percentage meters, animated counters, tag clouds.
  • Scroll animations, fade-ins, parallax, typewriter effects.
  • A second accent colour. A "Let's connect!" call to action.

Contrast

Body text on ground is 15.6:1 in light and 14.1:1 in dark. The faintest text (--fg-faint) clears 4.5:1 in both, so dates and captions are readable, not decorative. If any change drops a text colour below 4.5:1, the change is wrong.

Newsreader is a variable serif drawn for screen reading — it is the prose face and the heading face. Public Sans handles navigation, descriptions and anything tabular. JetBrains Mono is code and the metadata lines. All three are installed from npm via Fontsource and served from your own domain, so the site makes no third-party font request and works offline.

I work on making large systems behave — first the distributed infrastructure, then the monitoring around it, and now the cost and reliability of AI agents.

Bodysix paragraphs

Right now I lead a small team working on reducing the cost of agentic workflows without sacrificing task quality. We are developing an uncertainty-aware router that decides at each step whether a smaller open-weight model is likely to be sufficient or whether the task should be escalated to a larger model. The routing logic itself is only part of the problem. Much of the work is in designing controlled experiments that measure the savings and the effect on task success reliably. We are also working on integrating this into llm-d, with the aim of making it a practical capability for people running open-weight models rather than leaving it as an experimental result.

Before that, I spent roughly a decade working on cloud systems. I helped build Watson Developer Cloud from its first open beta through its subsequent growth, and worked with a team of two to four engineers to operate its core distributed services with close to zero downtime. That experience shaped how I think about operational systems: monitoring should help people identify problems, not page them whenever a metric moves.

I later moved into AIOps, working on anomaly detection and root-cause analysis over logs and metrics. My team put this into production together with the group operating IBM Cloud’s network. I also built a release-verification system that detects when a change to a machine training pipeline degrades customers’ chatbot models before the change is released.

More recently, I led LakeGuard, a project on access control for structured and vector data in GenAI lakehouses. We took permissions originating in systems such as Box and SharePoint, mapped them into a common model, and enforced them across Iceberg, Milvus, Presto and Postgres while exploring how to carefully manage query latency.

I have a PhD from UCL on data-driven detection and diagnosis of failures in service compositions, six granted patents, and papers at EuroSys, ICSE, WWW and ICSOC. Earlier in my career, I was an Eclipse committer on the BPEL Designer, worked on Grid workflow tooling at UCL, and started out at Goldman Sachs, where I learned how much a production release can actually cost.

The common thread in my work is curiosity about difficult technical problems and a preference for problems that matter in practice. I am most at home when a problem is still poorly defined: turning it into questions that can be tested, working through the evidence, and then building something that holds up in practice. I like small teams where people trust and support one another, feel safe disagreeing openly, and keep getting better at designing experiments that tell us early when we are wrong.

§ 6

On tone — is this too immodest?

No, and I would not turn it down. The draft is not immodest; it is specific, and those are different things that are easy to confuse when you are reading about yourself.

Boasting is adjectives about the person: world-class, passionate, visionary, thought leader. Read the draft again and count them — there are none. What is actually there is nouns and verbs about what happened: close to zero downtime with a team of two to four; adopted in production by the team running IBM Cloud's network; six granted patents. Those are checkable facts, and a checkable fact is not a brag no matter how good it looks. The reader supplies the admiration, which is the only kind that lands.

That also means there is no way to make the page more modest without deleting the specifics, because the specifics are all there is to delete. And vagueness is the thing that actually costs opportunities. A reader who cannot tell what you did assumes one of two things — that it was small, or that you cannot articulate it — and neither of those gets you an email.

Where modesty earns its place

In the framing, not the facts — and the draft already spends it there. "The unglamorous end of cloud." "The routing is the easy part." "Being wrong early." Understated framing wrapped around precise facts is the register that reads as senior: confident about the work, light about yourself. The inverse — grand framing around vague facts — is what reads as junior, and it is the far more common failure.

The thing to actually watch for is not arrogance. It is the CV voice: the sentence that lists credentials with no verb in it. There is exactly one of those in the draft, and it is discussed below.

If you want to bring more opportunities your way

Tone is close to irrelevant here compared with two other things.

  • A concrete claim beats both modesty and confidence. The most valuable sentence on a personal site is one a reader can act on — "I built this, it does that, here is the number". You have several and they are the reason to keep the page specific. If anything, add: how many clusters, how much alert volume fell, how many tasks the routing experiment covered. Numbers you are comfortable publishing are worth more than any adjustment to register.
  • The writing is the engine, not the bio. A page that says you work on agent cost optimisation is worth much less than a post that shows you thinking about it in public. Nobody has ever emailed someone because of an "About" page; people email because of a post that was useful to them. The bio's job is to be accurate and get out of the way — which is roughly what this draft does — and then the blog does the actual work.

Two lines worth a second look

These are the two most assertive passages. Both alternatives are genuinely available; my recommendation is on each.

A — the monitoring linekeep as drafted

As drafted: “That taught me the thing that has shaped everything since: if you page a human every time a metric wobbles, you do not have monitoring, you have noise.”

Quieter: “That is where I learned that most monitoring problems are not detection problems — by the time an SRE has started ignoring the alerts, adding more of them makes it worse.”

Keep the original. It is the most memorable sentence on the page, and it is a strong claim about systems, not about you — which is exactly where a strong claim belongs. The quieter version is fine writing but it hedges the one opinion the page has.

B — the credentials sentencetake the quieter version

As drafted: “I have a PhD from UCL on data-driven detection and diagnosis of failures in service compositions, six granted patents, and papers at EuroSys, ICSE, WWW and ICSOC.”

Quieter: “The PhD was at UCL, on detecting and diagnosing failures in distributed service compositions. Some of what came after ended up as patents and papers; they are on the publications page.”

Take the quieter one. This is the CV voice — a list with no verb — and it is the only sentence on the page where the enumeration is the point. Moving the list to the page that exists for lists makes the home page read better and sends the interested reader somewhere, which the original does not. Nothing is lost: the patents and papers are one click away and fully credited.

If you change nothing else

Apply B, leave A, and stop editing. Re-reading your own bio past the second pass reliably makes it blander, because every specific claim starts to feel like a boast on the fifth reading and none of them did on the first — which is the only reading anyone else will give it.
